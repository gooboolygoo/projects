Read first — five honest caveats

Civilizations ran in parallel. Until about 1500 they rose side by side with no shared yardstick. Each one’s own ladder is shown here; comparing a rank in Rome to one in Tang China is rough.

The unwritten 98%. Before farming, people lived in small, largely equal bands. Standing was real but personal, and left almost no record.

Rank is not admirable. This ranks reach and sway, not virtue. Several of the most powerful people here caused immense suffering.

Each civilization’s hierarchy and success-traits are cited under it; every figure links to its Grokipedia article. Grokipedia is AI-generated — a starting point to check, not a settled record.

The percentages are estimates. Each tier shows an approximate share of that society’s population — sourced where Grokipedia or a standard reference gives a figure, reasoned where they don’t. Exact demographics of dead societies are unknowable and the strata overlap, so they may not sum to exactly 100. The bar length is drawn in proportion to that share — which is why the named apex is a hairline and the base fills the field; the color and the order, top to base, mark the rank. The shape — a sliver of elite over a vast base — is what is firmly attested.

On sources. The status hierarchy and the traits that improved one’s odds of rising are drawn for each civilization from Grokipedia, supplemented by standard historical references (e.g. J. M. Roberts, A History of the World) where Grokipedia is thin; the works used are linked or cited beneath each. Each named figure links to its Grokipedia article. Exact demographics of vanished societies are unknowable; the tiers and any shares are informed estimates, and the shape — a sliver of elite over a vast base — is what is firmly attested.

Two things to notice. First, brightness in the record runs opposite to numbers: the layer we can name is the thinnest capstone, while the base that grew the food and built the walls is nameless. Second, read the tag on each civilization — across almost every one, the single most reliable route to the top was simply being born to the right parents. The exceptions stand out: a few merit ladders (China’s exams, the Mongols, the Ottoman devshirme, the Napoleonic career), and, only at the present end, wealth.
